# Emotion and context

Use this reference when choosing an emotional direction, shaping a journey, or resolving competing design ideas. These are design hypotheses to adapt, not promises that a feeling will cause conversion.

## Connect the feeling to an experience

Distinguish the desired outcome (finish a project), the need it may serve (competence), a possible feeling (pride), and the observable behavior (publish the project). None alone proves the others. Start with the audience's own language when research is available.

| Emotional direction | Concrete ways to explore it | What changes with context |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Curiosity and anticipation | An interactive preview, a revealing first action, a personally relevant next possibility | Exploration benefits from discovery; urgent tasks need a clear route to resolution |
| Confidence and relief | A workable plan, comprehensible choices, transparent status, effective recovery | A newcomer may need guidance; an expert may value speed and control |
| Competence and pride | Meaningful participation, specific feedback, evidence of increasing independence | Learning may benefit from challenge; routine administration may benefit from fewer demands |
| Ownership and identity | An editable creation, useful preferences, a personal collection, continuity of work | Personalization should contribute to the task; account or data requirements may precede a result |
| Connection and recognition | Relevant examples, useful remembered context, attribution of contributions, collaboration | Private work can feel understood without community features; social products may depend on reciprocity between people |
| Ambition and possibility | A credible demonstration of the next capability or expanded outcome | Some users want mastery or growth; others want a stable routine or a finished task |

Emotion is shaped by pacing, hierarchy, visual character, sound, motion, and microcopy as well as by features. Choose an expressive treatment that serves the moment: anticipation before a reveal, specific recognition after an accomplishment, calm clarity during recovery. Let the actual action give the feedback substance. A decorative treatment alone does not establish the emotional response.

## Follow the promise through time

Before a commitment, explore what makes the outcome imaginable and credible. During first use, make a useful payoff perceptible. At completion, help the person understand what changed and what they can do next. For a return visit, connect previous value to a relevant new or recurring purpose. For an interruption, account for changed circumstances, forgotten context, or a completed need.

This is a lens for locating moments that matter. A brief task can have one such moment; a collaborative product may have different promises for the creator, invitee, and administrator. The person who pays may also differ from the person who uses the feature.

## Resolve tensions with a reason

| Tension | Decision to make |
| --- | --- |
| Defaults and personal investment | Offer a useful starting point; invite decisions whose consequences matter to this person |
| Ease and meaningful effort | Reduce administrative work while preserving participation that creates value; adjust help to the obstacle |
| Novelty and familiarity | Decide where discovery motivates and where predictability makes returning easier |
| Satisfaction and anticipation | Deliver a worthwhile stopping point and make an appropriate next possibility visible |
| Early value and required commitment | Consider whether value can be experienced, previewed, or credibly explained before authentication, permissions, or payment |
| Immediate conversion and continuing value | Examine whether the change attracts people likely to benefit and whether later outcomes support the apparent win |
| Engagement and independence | Determine whether more visits, less effort, task completion, or successful activity elsewhere best serves the goal |

Avoid resolving these by automatically maximizing one side. Explain the choice in terms of the current audience and outcome. When evidence is thin, propose a small way to distinguish plausible explanations; do not diagnose feelings from appearance alone.

## Research grounding

- Hassenzahl, Diefenbach & Göritz (2010), [Needs, affect, and interactive products](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.intcom.2010.04.002), studied reports of positive experiences and their relationship to need fulfilment and product evaluation. This supports exploring needs such as competence, relatedness, and stimulation; it does not establish an interface-specific conversion effect. The author's [experience-design resources](https://hassenzahl.wordpress.com/experience-design-tools/) connect needs to possible feelings and interaction qualities.
- Ryan, Rigby & Przybylski (2006), [The motivational pull of video games](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2006_RyanRigbyPrzybylski_MandE.pdf), examined competence, autonomy, and relatedness in game experiences, including associations with enjoyment and future-play intentions. Use it as a source for motivational hypotheses; distinguish intended use from observed product retention and account for the different product setting.
